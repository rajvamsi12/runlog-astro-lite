## The problem

Most self-driving research is trained and tested on structured traffic: clear lanes, predictable merges. A junction in Gwalior has none of that. Auto-rickshaws cut across, bikes squeeze through gaps, cars overtake on either side.

A policy that has never seen this will fail on it. I saw that first-hand when a behavior cloning model I'd trained drove straight into a parked auto-rickshaw in simulation. To train and test on real Indian driving, I needed that driving inside a simulator.

## The idea

Record real traffic from a drone, track every vehicle, and replay those exact trajectories in CARLA. Then use the replays to train an AI driver that has to drive from its own sensors, the way a real car would - and check whether it behaves like the Gwalior drivers it learned from.

## Part 1: real traffic inside the simulator

1. **Map the real junction onto a CARLA map.** I split the junction into 6 regions (each approach road) plus the center, and calibrated each region separately onto Town10HD. A corrected mapping mirrors left-hand traffic onto CARLA's right-hand lanes: 98.2% of moving steps now go with the CARLA lane, up from 3.9%.
2. **Spawn matching vehicles.** Each real vehicle gets a CARLA blueprint of similar size, placed aligned with the road.
3. **Replay with physics.** A time-synchronized PID controller drives each vehicle along its real path. Tracking error dropped from 5.9 m to under 0.3 m.
4. **Throw out bad data.** Drone tracking has glitches - speed jumps, heading flips, drift. A filter drops those scenarios, and an offline predictor flags the ones that would collide before CARLA ever runs them.
5. **Make recovery examples.** Copies of each scenario add scene noise, shift the ego car off its path, or change its speed, so the driver also learns how to get back on track.

## Part 2: an AI driver that learns from it

The driver sees what a real car would: three recent front-camera frames, three LiDAR sweeps, its own speed and steering, and a route command. Camera features come from SegFormer and LiDAR features from PointPillars. A GRU decoder predicts the next 2.5 seconds of waypoints as corrections to a constant-velocity guess. I pick the checkpoint by how it drives in closed loop, not by its offline error.

## Results

I test every driver in closed loop on 114 test scenarios it has never seen, 10 runs each. "Behaviour kept" checks whether the ego still does what the real driver did - passes the same car on the same side, or keeps the same gap. A TTC (time-to-collision) safety layer brakes when a crash is predicted within 0.7 s.

| Driver | Ego collisions | Behaviour kept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recorded driver (replayed) | 2.5% | 100% |
| AI driver alone | 31.0% | 58.4% |
| **AI driver + TTC** | **1.4%** | **62.0%** |
| Constant velocity + TTC | 1.2% | 61.8% |
| IDM car-following model | 25.6% | 74.6% |
| CARLA Autopilot (recorded path and speed) | 9.1% | 68.8% |

What this shows, honestly: the safety layer removes almost all crashes, but the AI driver with it does about as well as a simple constant-velocity driver with the same layer. A probe where the real driver drives and the model only predicts explains why - when the real driver slows down, the model predicts only 5.5% of that slow-down. It has learned to keep going, not to brake.

## What's next

DAgger-style data, where the model drives and the expert's correction is recorded, to teach braking and recovery. This work is also the basis of my first-author paper submitted to WAFR 2026.

## Where it started

The thesis grew out of my iHub-Data (IIIT Hyderabad) internship and B.Tech project, [Drone to Ego](/projects/drone-to-ego). There I built the first version: drone scenes rebuilt in CARLA, spawn correction, time-to-collision based collision avoidance, and a SegFormer-B1 behavior cloning model trained on about 66,000 image-command pairs (0.69 m/s speed error, 4.89°/s steering error).

That project showed how much the data itself was holding the model back. The thesis rebuilds the pipeline around that: six calibrated regions instead of five, filtered and validated scenarios, collisions caught before simulation, and a driver that uses LiDAR as well as the camera.
